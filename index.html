Australia's Healthcare Workforce

Who is growing, where they work, and what it means for access to care

Australia has more registered health professionals than it did four years ago. But a larger national workforce does not automatically mean every profession or region benefits equally. This story follows the workforce from national growth to the state and remote-area patterns hidden underneath it.

01

A growing workforce

Growth is clear, but it is not evenly shared

The national registered workforce increased from 642,479 in 2020 to 757,517 in 2024. Nursing and midwifery dominates the workforce by size, while several allied-health professions grew faster proportionally.

02

Professions are changing differently

Headcount alone does not describe workforce pressure

Growth, working hours and workforce age reveal different pressures. A fast-growing workforce may still work long hours, while an older workforce can raise future replacement concerns.

03

Geography changes the picture

Workforce size and workforce availability tell different stories

New South Wales and Victoria have the largest workforces because they also have large populations. Dividing full-time-equivalent workers by population provides a fairer state comparison.

What does full-time equivalent mean? Full-time equivalent converts reported working hours into a comparable workload measure. Here, full-time-equivalent workers per 100,000 residents is used as an availability proxy—not a direct measure of healthcare quality or unmet demand.

04

Beyond major cities

Distance changes both distribution and workload

About three quarters of the published workforce is located in major cities. Outside those cities, workforce numbers fall sharply while average hours rise for several essential professions.

The central lesson

More workers do not automatically mean equal access

Australia added more than 115,000 registered health professionals between 2020 and 2024. Yet growth differed across professions, workforce supply differed across states, and reported hours rose in remote settings. Planning therefore needs to consider profession, workload, population and geography together—not national headcount alone.

Methods and limitations

How to read this visualisation

Population matching

State rates combine 2024 workforce full-time-equivalent counts with Australian Bureau of Statistics estimated resident population at 31 December 2024. Using a matching reference year avoids mixing newer population figures with older workforce figures.

What is included

The data describes registered professionals who reported being employed in Australia. It does not represent every healthcare worker, vacancy, service, patient outcome or level of demand.

Missing values

Values withheld for confidentiality are retained as missing values and never treated as zero. Published subtotals may therefore differ slightly from visible components.

Survey and processing

The National Health Workforce Dataset combines annual Australian Health Practitioner Regulation Agency registration information with a voluntary workforce survey. The overall 2024 response rate was 94.1%, followed by imputation, geocoding and quality assurance.
